## 1. What are the established procedural plate-tectonics methods, and how does each one work?

### Takeaway
Only three families actually simulate moving plates over time: (a) Cortial et al. 2019 (rigid plates on a sphere, phenomenological rules for subduction, collision, ridges and rifting, 2 My steps, about 125 steps per planet); (b) Viitanen's PlaTec and its fork `plate-tectonics` / WorldEngine (per-plate rasters sliding on a wrapping flat grid, crust-mass bookkeeping, restart cycles); (c) Tectonics.js v2 (field-based, crust age drives density drives motion). Everything else in the hobby space (Experilous, Frozen Fractal, Undiscovered Worlds) is static: plates drawn once, boundaries classified once, no time. Cortial is the only one that records orogeny age and type per sample, which is exactly what the owner asks for.

### Cited Findings

**Cortial, Peytavie, Galin, Guérin — "Procedural Tectonic Planets" (Computer Graphics Forum / Eurographics 2019)**
- Explicitly phenomenological: "heuristics chosen for their performance and plausibility", "without guaranteed physical accuracy"; validated informally by one tectonics expert. — [Cortial et al. 2019, author PDF](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Representation: point samples on a sphere (Fibonacci sampling), one global spherical Delaunay triangulation partitioned into plates; values interpolated barycentrically. Per-sample crust attributes: crust type `xC` (oceanic / continental), thickness `e`, elevation `z`; for oceanic crust: age `ao` and local ridge direction `r`; for continental crust: **orogeny age `ac`, orogeny type `o` (Andean = from subduction, Himalayan = from continental collision), local fold direction `f`**. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Initial state: N plate centroids, plates = spherical Voronoi cells, boundaries warped by noise; continental/oceanic ratio and plate count are user inputs; each plate gets a random (or user-set) rigid rotation: axis `w` through the planet centre, angular speed `ω`, surface speed `s(p) = ω w × p`. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Time step `δt = 2 My`. "Realistic planets are obtained after a few hundreds iteration, i.e. ≈ 250 My" (250 My at 2 My is 125 steps; the paper's wording is loose). — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Subduction rule: of two converging oceanic plates the OLDER one subducts; oceanic always subducts under continental; continent–continent starts as "forced subduction" and then becomes a collision. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Subduction uplift on the overriding plate, per step: `ũ(p) = u0 · f(d(p)) · g(v(p)) · h(z̃_i(p))`, then `z += ũ·δt`. `d` = distance to the subduction front; `f` = piecewise cubic with a peak at a control distance, fading to 0 at the maximum subduction distance `rs`; `g(v) = v/v0` (relative plate speed); `h(z̃) = z̃²` with `z̃ = (z_i − zt)/(zc − zt)` the normalised elevation of the SUBDUCTING plate (high relief going down, e.g. plateaus or islands, deforms the upper plate more). Orogeny age of affected samples is reset to 0; fold direction is updated `f += β (s_i − s_j) δt`. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Slab pull: the subducting plate's rotation axis is nudged toward its subduction front each step: `w_i += ε Σ_k (c_i × q_k)/‖c_i × q_k‖ δt` over front points `q_k`, with `ε ≪ 1` so that only long fronts matter. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Continental collision is a DISCRETE event. A terrane (= any connected region of continental crust on a plate, island to whole continent) that interpenetrates another plate's continental crust by more than a threshold (300 km in their system) is detached from its plate and sutured to the other ("slab break"). Radius of influence `r = rc · sqrt(v(q)/v0 · A/A0)` (A = terrane area, A0 = average initial plate area, q = terrane centroid). Instant elevation surge `Δz(p) = Δc · A · (1 − (d(p,R)/r)²)²`; fold direction set perpendicular to the direction from the terrane centroid. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Oceanic crust generation: new samples between diverging plates get `z = α·z̄ + (1−α)·zΓ`, where `zΓ` is a template ridge profile, `z̄` the elevation interpolated between the plate borders, `α = dΓ/(dΓ + dP)` (distance to ridge vs distance to the nearest plate); ridge direction stored; new samples assigned to the nearest plate. Done only at global resampling, every 10–60 steps depending on maximum plate speed. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Rifting: discrete event, a plate is cut into n ∈ [2,4] sub-plates by random centroids + noise-warped Voronoi; fragments get random diverging directions. Triggered with probability `P = λ e^{−λ}`, `λ = λ0 · f(xP) · A/A0` (bigger and more continental plates rift more). Stated purpose: it "prevents the formation of unnatural super-continents caused by collisions". — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Per-step damping: continental erosion `z −= (z/zc) εc δt`; oceanic dampening `z −= (1 − z/zt) εo δt`; trench sediment fill `z += εt δt`; then low-frequency noise. Justified by the coarse resolution (≈50 km) and long steps. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Constants (Appendix A): δt 2 My; R 6370 km; highest ridge zr −1 km; abyssal plain za −6 km; trench zt −10 km; highest continental altitude zc 10 km; subduction distance rs 1800 km; collision distance rc 4200 km; collision coefficient Δc 1.3×10⁻⁵ km⁻¹; maximum plate speed v0 100 mm/y; oceanic damping εo 4×10⁻² mm/y; continental erosion εc 3×10⁻² mm/y; sediment accretion εt 3×10⁻¹ mm/y; subduction uplift u0 6×10⁻¹ mm/y. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Implementation: no remeshing per step — plates move rigidly as separate triangulations, boundary triangles are tracked for intersections with a bounding-box hierarchy per plate, and the WHOLE sphere is resampled onto the fixed point set every 10–60 iterations (the paper also says rifting and crust generation run "every 20 to 120 My"). Per-sample updates are multi-threaded. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Timings (C++, i7-6700K 4 GHz, 16 GB; 40 initial plates, land ratio 0.3), seconds per time step:

  | samples (resolution) | subduction | collision | elevation | total / step | oceanic crust (per resample) | rifting (per event) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 60k (102 km) | 0.08 | 0.02 | 0.09 | 0.19 | 0.58 | 0.23 |
  | 100k (78 km) | 0.14 | 0.04 | 0.10 | 0.28 | 1.22 | 0.21 |
  | 250k (50 km) | 0.26 | 0.69 | 0.29 | 1.24 | 3.58 | 1.50 |
  | 500k (35 km) | 0.65 | 0.63 | 0.62 | 1.90 | 13.10 | 7.70 |

  — [Cortial et al. 2019, Table 2](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Stated limitations: rifting separates fragments directly instead of tearing progressively; "tends to generate more forced subduction events than expected"; no passive margins / continental shelves; no hotspots (suggested fix: "specific sample points drifting across plates"); control only through plate motion and rifting. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Final relief is NOT produced by the tectonic model: the coarse crust is "amplified" — oceans by oriented Gabor noise using `r` and `ao`, continents by blending real SRTM 90 m exemplars chosen by terrain type and rotated to the fold direction. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)

**Viitanen — PlaTec (2012 B.Eng. thesis, Metropolia) and the `plate-tectonics` fork; WorldEngine**
- Origin: PlaTec was Lauri Viitanen's Bachelor of Engineering thesis (Metropolia, Helsinki); `Mindwerks/plate-tectonics` is a C++ fork with Python (PyPlatec) and Haskell bindings; WorldEngine and Widelands use it. — [plate-tectonics README](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics)
- Representation (from the code): a global heightmap `hmap`, plate-index map `imap` and age map `amap`, plus ONE RASTER PER PLATE (its own bounding rectangle, height map and crust-timestamp map) that slides over the world grid. Height doubles as crust mass. `CONTINENTAL_BASE = 1.0` separates continental from oceanic crust; `OCEANIC_BASE = 0.1` is the height of fresh ocean floor. — [lithosphere.hpp / lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- Initialisation: a fractal heightmap, thresholded at `sea_level` into continental (h + CONTINENTAL_BASE) and oceanic (OCEANIC_BASE) crust; plates grown from random seed cells by random border flood fill; the grid wraps in BOTH axes (a torus) — neighbour and coordinate lookups are modulo width and height. — [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- Motion: each plate has a 2D velocity in a random direction with `INITIAL_SPEED_X = 1` (cells per step) — translation is what I verified; whether its rotation code has any visible effect was not checked; plates are point masses with a centre of mass; continental collision applies an impulse with coefficient of restitution 0, and friction `DEFORMATION_WEIGHT (2) × deformed_mass / mass` slows them. — [movement.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/movement.cpp)
- Per step, each plate is stamped onto the world grid. Where two plates overlap a cell: if the incoming crust is oceanic and the resident is "buoyant" (higher, or equal and younger) the incoming cell SUBDUCTS — `OCEANIC_BASE` is removed from it and "sediment" `SUBDUCT_RATIO (0.5) × OCEANIC_BASE × (CONTINENTAL_BASE − h)/CONTINENTAL_BASE` is queued for the overriding plate (more sediment the deeper the water above the subducting crust). If both are continental, `folding_ratio × h` of the lower crust is transferred to the other plate each step (folding) and the overlap is counted as a continental collision. — [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- Aggregation (suture): when the collision count between two continents exceeds `aggr_overlap_abs` cells or the overlapped fraction exceeds `aggr_overlap_rel`, the whole colliding continent SEGMENT (found by flood fill) is moved from one plate to the other and the plates' momenta are combined. — [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- Slab pull is faked: "Do not apply friction to oceanic plates. This is a very cheap way to emulate slab pull." — [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- Divergence: cells left empty after the move are filled with new oceanic crust at `OCEANIC_BASE × BUOYANCY_BONUS_X (3)`, owned by "that neighbour plate who was located at this point before plates moved"; young oceanic crust keeps a buoyancy bonus that fades over `MAX_BUOYANCY_AGE = 20` steps (this is what draws the mid-ocean ridge). — [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- Erosion: every `erosion_period` steps each plate runs a simple downhill-transfer erosion with 10 % random noise added to the heightmap. — [plate.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/plate.cpp)
- There is NO rifting model. Instead the simulation RESTARTS: when total velocity < `RESTART_SPEED_LIMIT` (2.0), kinetic energy falls below `RESTART_ENERGY_RATIO` (0.15) of its peak, no continental collision happened for `NO_COLLISION_TIME_LIMIT` (10) steps, or `RESTART_ITERATIONS` (600) steps passed, the plates are discarded, the current heightmap is re-cut into fresh random plates with fresh random velocities, and a new cycle starts; `cycle_count` cycles in total. — [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- WorldEngine's defaults: `sea_level=0.65, erosion_period=60, folding_ratio=0.02, aggr_overlap_abs=1000000, aggr_overlap_rel=0.33, cycle_count=2, num_plates=10`; it steps until `is_finished`. — [worldengine/plates.py](https://github.com/Mindwerks/worldengine/blob/master/worldengine/plates.py)
- Secondhand (a GameDev.net reader quoting the thesis' benchmark table, not verified against the thesis): on a Pentium 4, about 8–15 s for 128² up to 300–480 s for 1024². — [GameDev.net thread](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/623145-terrain-generation-with-plate-tectonics/)
- Thesis location (could not be fetched in this session): — [Viitanen 2012, Theseus](https://www.theseus.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/40422/Viitanen_Lauri_2012_03_30.pdf)

**Tectonics.js (Carl Davidson) and its predecessor PyTectonics**
- v1 was "generate a series of plates at random; generate a series of velocities at random; destroy crust whenever intersections occur" — which the author says "is how its done in virtually every other non-academic tectonics simulator I've seen, and it's wrong": it left ocean crust a billion years old, whereas on Earth almost no ocean crust is older than about 250 My. — [Tectonics.js blog, "Version 2.0"](https://github.com/davidson16807/tectonics.js/blob/master/_posts/2017-9-16-version2.md)
- v2 inverts the design ("ocean crust must die"): the model tracks crust age, derives density from age, marks crust for subduction when density passes a threshold, computes a velocity field (with vector calculus over scalar/vector fields on the sphere grid, not a Navier–Stokes solve) that pulls crust toward subducting regions, segments that field into plates by image segmentation (flood fill of similar velocities), gives each plate the weighted average velocity, deletes overlapped subducting crust and fills gaps with new crust (ridges). Plates are therefore an OUTPUT of the density field, not an input. — [Tectonics.js blog](https://davidson16807.github.io/tectonics.js/blog); [Version 2.0 post](https://github.com/davidson16807/tectonics.js/blob/master/_posts/2017-9-16-version2.md)
- Erosion in Tectonics.js is a height-difference transfer between neighbouring cells scaled by precipitation (after Simoes et al. 2010); before it existed mountains "eventually occup[ied] every available cell of continental crust", and after it mountains were still far too tall ("say, 15 km"). — [Tectonics.js blog, "Reddit and erosion"](https://github.com/davidson16807/tectonics.js/blob/master/_posts/2014-1-29-reddit-erosion-photosynthesis.md)
- Scale: built for a browser at "tens of thousands of grid cells"; an early build ran about 10,000 cells at 20 fps single-threaded; rifting and collision dominated run time in the Python predecessor. — [Tectonics.js blog](https://davidson16807.github.io/tectonics.js/blog); [first post](https://github.com/davidson16807/tectonics.js/blob/master/_posts/2013-12-1-tectonics-now-in-your-browser.md)

**second-system.de (2022) — a hobby re-implementation of Cortial with a different motion model**
- One coherent Delaunay mesh for the whole planet (not one triangulation per plate); each vertex is a "sub-plate" with plate id, elevation, crust type, velocity, crust age; motion is a damped-spring soft body (Verlet): same-plate edges are springs, divergent edges push (ridge push), ocean–continent and ocean–ocean convergent edges subduct the denser side and apply slab pull, continent–continent edges resist. — [Plate Tectonics 1, second-system.de](https://second-system.de/2022/03/01/tectonics_1)
- The author notes Cortial "doesn't describe plate movement in detail", and reports artifacts from edge flips (continents deform, land area is lost), fixed with dual-edge springs; demo scale was only about 1,300 vertices. — [second-system.de](https://second-system.de/2022/03/01/tectonics_1)

**Static (non-simulated) plate generators**
- Frozen Fractal "Around the World, part 2": about 15 plates grown from 6 continents on a flat map, one velocity per plate, each boundary segment classified once (convergent/divergent × crust types) and a rule applied per class (C–C convergent: mountains + plateau, stronger with speed; C–C divergent: rift valley; O–O divergent: ridge; O–O convergent: island arc; O–C convergent: trench + volcanic arc). No time stepping. Mountains looked too smooth until noise modulated width and height. — [Frozen Fractal 2023](https://frozenfractal.com/blog/2023/11/13/around-the-world-2-plate-tectonics/)
- Experilous (Andy Gainey, 2014) is the reference for this static family; Red Blob Games' planet generator states it follows "the approach of Andy Gainey and others to build tectonic plates". Only secondhand descriptions were reachable here (random seeds + round-robin flood fill for plates; boundary types; elevation spread inward from boundaries). — [Red Blob Games, 1843 planet generation](https://www.redblobgames.com/x/1843-planet-generation/)
- Undiscovered Worlds does not simulate plates: continents are pasted "blobs", mountain ranges are drawn as lines afterwards (sometimes along a continent edge to imitate subduction), and mountains are stored separately from base elevation. — [Undiscovered Worlds blog](https://undiscoveredworlds.blogspot.com/2019/02/basic-continents.html); [GitHub README](https://github.com/fegennari/Undiscovered_Worlds)

**Academic follow-ups 2016–2024 (uplift → erosion, not plate motion)**
- Cordonnier et al. 2016, "Large Scale Terrain Generation from Tectonic Uplift and Fluvial Erosion": a user-painted UPLIFT MAP drives the stream power equation on a stream graph; this is the graphics paper that introduced the uplift-map + stream-power pipeline (Braun is a co-author). — [Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf12820)
- Schott, Paris, Fournier, Guérin, Galin 2023 — actual title "Large-scale terrain authoring through interactive erosion simulation" (ACM TOG 42(5), art. 162), not "…landscape evolution": terrain is authored through the uplift map, relief is computed by stream-power erosion with a fast approximation of drainage area and flow routing; tools include copy-paste, warping for folds and faults, point/curve elevation constraints, and reconstruction of an uplift map from a given elevation model. Code is public. — [project page](https://h-schott.github.io/publications/uplift/publi_uplift.html); [code](https://github.com/H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3592787)
- Tzathas, Gailleton, Steer, Cordonnier 2024, "Physically-based analytical erosion for fast terrain generation" (CGF 43(2)): stream power law solved analytically for large mountain ranges, with TIME as an input parameter, "from early erosion of the input terrain to steady state between uplift and erosion". — [Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf15033)

### Inferences
- A 250 My Cortial run at 250k samples (about the cell count of a 512² grid) costs roughly 125 × 1.24 s + about 5–10 resamplings × 3.6 s ≈ 3 minutes on a 2015 quad-core; at 500k samples about 125 × 1.9 + 5–10 × 13 s ≈ 5–6 minutes. Their collision and resampling costs grow faster than linearly, so 2048² (4.2 M cells) would not fit "minutes" with their mesh approach; a raster implementation has no triangulation or BVH and should scale linearly. (My arithmetic from Table 2; not a reported figure.)
- PlaTec's weaknesses for this project, read from the code: plates essentially translate on a torus, no rifting (restarts with a fresh random cut are the substitute), no recorded orogeny age/type (only a crust creation timestamp), mountains are literally piled crust mass with no uplift rate, and the simulation "ends" when plates stall. Its strength is the data structure: per-plate rasters on a wrapping grid are trivial, fast and lossless under translation.
- Tectonics.js v2's key idea worth stealing is causal direction: old dense ocean crust decides where subduction starts and thereby where plates go. Cortial's "older oceanic plate subducts" + slab-pull axis nudge is a cheap version of the same idea.
- Cortial's exemplar amplification is irrelevant here: the project already has a stream-power stage, which is the Cordonnier/Schott route. What transfers is the crust attribute set and the event rules.

## 2. How does each method produce continents, shelves, island arcs, ridges, trenches and hotspot chains?

### Takeaway
In every simulator continents are an initial condition (a fraction of crust flagged continental) that is then reshaped: Cortial splits them by Poisson rifting and merges them by terrane suturing; PlaTec merges by aggregation and re-cuts on restart; Tectonics.js lets continental crust persist while ocean crust is recycled. Ridges, trenches and island arcs fall out of the boundary rules in Cortial; shelves and hotspots are modelled by none of them.

### Cited Findings
- Cortial: continental vs oceanic is a per-sample flag with an initial land ratio (0.3 in the benchmark, 40 initial plates); continents break by rifting events whose rate grows with plate area and continental fraction; they grow/merge when terranes suture in collisions. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Cortial island arcs and accretion: ocean–ocean subduction uplifts the overriding sea floor until it emerges as an island arc (orogeny age set to 0 for emerging parts); in their Figure 15 an arc appears at 330 My, becomes a large island by 340 My and is fully accreted to a continent at 370 My after the plate is redirected. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Cortial ridges, abyssal plains, trenches: template ridge profile at −1 km blended toward the plate borders; oceanic dampening pulls ageing crust down toward the −6 km plain; trenches reach −10 km and are slowly filled by sediment accretion. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Cortial omissions: "we did not model passive margins and more generally shallow underwater continental parts" and "we did not generate hot spots … such as the Hawaiian islands chain. This feature could be easily implemented in our model as specific sample points drifting across plates." — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- A supercontinent plus rifting scenario is shown as USER-driven: collision makes a supercontinent at 191 My, the user triggers a rift, a new sea exists at 245 My; a rifted pair is visibly separated after 15 My. — [Cortial et al. 2019, Figs. 10 and 16](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- PlaTec: continents come from thresholding a fractal map at `sea_level` (WorldEngine 0.65); ridges come from the buoyancy bonus on young crust (3× base, fading over 20 steps); subduction deposits sediment on the overriding plate (arc/coastal range); continents merge by aggregation; the supercontinent cycle is imitated by restart cycles (`cycle_count`, WorldEngine 2). — [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp); [worldengine/plates.py](https://github.com/Mindwerks/worldengine/blob/master/worldengine/plates.py)
- Real-world rules of thumb collected by Worldbuilding Pasta (a hobbyist synthesis of the geology literature, not a primary source): divergent boundaries almost always START inside continents; rifts start at triple junctions and the third arm fails (aulacogen) — a weak, easily eroded zone that often becomes a major river valley or inlet and can re-rift later; rifts zig-zag AROUND cratons; passive margins get wide shelves and coastal plains; island arcs usually attach to a continent at one end; arcs, microcontinents and oceanic plateaus accrete onto continents and subduction then jumps outboard or flips polarity; only a major continent–continent collision reliably ends subduction; plumes sit preferentially under supercontinents. — [Worldbuilding Pasta, Part Va](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2020/01/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-va.html)
- Supercontinent timing (same source): about 750 My per cycle excluding Pannotia, possibly 400–500 My including it; Nuna ≈1.8 Ga, Rodinia ≈1 Ga, Pangea ≈250 Ma; suggested hand procedure: start from a supercontinent covering about a quarter of the planet with cratons marked, step in 50 My increments, break it with a rift that avoids cratons, ring it with subduction, let subduction decide plate motion with rifting and spreading as passive responses. — [Worldbuilding Pasta, Part Va](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2020/01/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-va.html)
- Hotspots and LIPs (same source): about 10–20 active hotspots; a dozen or so large continental igneous provinces in the last half-billion years. — [Worldbuilding Pasta, Part Va](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2020/01/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-va.html)

### Inferences
- "Several continents" is a rifting-vs-suturing balance. Cortial's Poisson rate λ0 is the dial: too low gives one supercontinent (the project's current symptom in another form), too high gives confetti. Starting from one supercontinent (plus cratons) and simulating the breakup gives jigsaw-fit continents with passive margins on the rifted sides and active margins on the outer sides — the Earth-like pattern — and is cheaper to control than starting from scattered random continents.
- Cratons can be a per-cell "strength" or "crust age" value that (a) rift lines avoid and (b) raises resistance to erosion later; neither simulator has it, but it costs one scalar field.
- Shelves are cheap to add after the fact: mark cells where continental crust was stretched at a rift (thin continental crust near a passive margin) and give them an elevation just below sea level; width as a function of margin age. This is my design suggestion, not from a source.
- Hotspot chains: a set of fixed points in the mantle frame; each step, stamp a volcanic uplift/age mark onto whatever plate cell is above. On a moving plate this draws an age-progressive chain for free (Cortial suggests the same in one sentence).

### Gaps
- No simulator found that models continental shelves, back-arc basins, hotspots or large igneous provinces; these are design work.
- No quantitative source obtained for typical shelf widths or for how fast a rift widens beyond the single Cortial figure (15 My to visible separation).

## 3. How can the simulation record age and type of each mountain belt, and what does geology say about relief vs age?

### Takeaway
Cortial already stores exactly this per sample — orogeny age, orogeny type (Andean/Himalayan) and fold direction — and switches to "old mountains" above an age threshold; extending the type enum and adding crust thickness is straightforward. Geology supports the young-sharp / old-rounded contrast but warns that a plain stream-power model erases relief in only 1–10 My after uplift stops, so old ranges need isostatic rebound, erosion thresholds or resistant rock to survive for hundreds of My.

### Cited Findings
- Cortial per-sample record: `ac` orogeny age (reset to 0 whenever subduction uplift or a collision affects the sample, otherwise ageing), `o` orogeny type ∈ {Andean (subduction), Himalayan (continental collision)}, `f` fold direction (accumulated from relative plate velocity in subduction; radial from the terrane centroid in a collision). — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Cortial's use of it: each continental sample gets a terrain type — Andean, Himalayan, "old mountains" if the local orogeny age exceeds a user threshold, or plain if outside any orogeny — and an exemplar of that type is placed and rotated to the fold direction. The paper says 19 exemplar sets were used and then lists 7 Himalayan + 11 Andean + 6 old (= 24; the paper's own numbers do not add up). — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Orogen classes used by geologists: accretionary (ocean subducting under a continent; Andes) vs collisional (two continental blocks; Himalaya); collisional ones subdivide by what collides — continent, continental fragment, or island arc; the Urals formed by Siberia and other terranes converging with Laurussia. — [Wikipedia, Orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orogeny)
- A practical four-way scheme (hobbyist synthesis): Andean (subduction along a continental coast), Laramide (flat-slab subduction from fast convergence or a subducted ridge — broad uplift far inland; Rockies), Ural (continent vs continent / microcontinent / arc, ordinary speed), Himalayan (several collisions in a row or one fast continent collision with one-sided subduction; gives the broad plateau). Present-day orogen widths range from about 50 km (Urals) to about 1,300 km (US Southwest). — [Worldbuilding Pasta, Part Va](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2020/01/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-va.html)
- Rule of thumb for height vs age (explicitly the blogger's own approximation, "exponential decay might fit better"): mean range height starts about 2,500 m and drops about 5 m per My; peak height starts about 4,000 m and drops about 8 m per My. — [Worldbuilding Pasta, Part Va](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2020/01/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-va.html)
- Appalachians: today's ranges are "eroded remnants of higher mountains" from continental collision about 300 My ago; the range likely once matched the Alps/Rockies (one geologist says Andes scale); it was worn to a near plain by the end of the Mesozoic and its present relief comes from Cenozoic re-uplift; highest point now about 2,037 m, mean about 900 m. — [US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/geology/plate-tectonics-collisional-mountain-ranges.htm); [Wikipedia, Geology of the Appalachians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_Appalachians)
- Decay time under plain stream power: the standard detachment-limited stream power model "predicts, for reasonable initial topographies, relatively short postorogenic decay times of 1–10 Myr". — [Baldwin, Whipple, Tucker 2003, JGR 108](https://doi.org/10.1029/2001JB000550)
- What stretches it (same study; multipliers from the 2001 conference abstract, to be re-checked against the paper): Airy isostatic rebound of the crustal root, at most about ×6; a transition to transport-limited conditions, about ×2–3; a critical shear stress (erosion threshold), about ×20 in their example; together these can explain residual topography lasting more than 100 My. — [GSA 2001 abstract](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2001ESP/webprogram/Paper7604.html)
- Old belts (Appalachians, Urals, Cape Fold Belt) keep significant relief tens to hundreds of My after tectonism; a post-orogenic model needed an erodibility threshold to slow decay. — [A model for post-orogenic development of a mountain range and its foreland (abstract)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263173840_A_model_for_post-orogenic_development_of_a_mountain_range_and_its_foreland)
- Old-range relief is partly lithological and rejuvenated, not just left over: contrasting rock types can GROW relief in a tectonically dead landscape; measured Appalachian erosion rates are about 5–30 m/My above knickpoints and 50–100 m/My below. — [ESurf 2021, Growing topography due to contrasting rock types](https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/9/167/2021/); [Penn State, Neogene rejuvenation of central Appalachian topography](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/neogene-rejuvenation-of-central-appalachian-topography-evidence-f/)
- Isostasy: mountains stand high because thickened crust floats higher; Airy model = constant-density crust of varying thickness with roots under high terrain; collision-zone crust reaches up to about 80 km versus about 40 km average continental crust. — [Wikipedia, Isostasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isostasy); [EBSCO research starter, Isostasy](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/earth-and-atmospheric-sciences/isostasy)

### Inferences
- Suggested per-cell record for this project (superset of Cortial's): `crust_kind` (oceanic/continental), `crust_age` (My since creation; doubles as craton indicator when very old), `crust_thickness`, `orogeny_age` (My since last active uplift), `orogeny_kind` enum {none, volcanic arc (ocean–ocean), Andean cordillera, collisional (Ural-style), collisional-plateau (Himalayan-style), accreted terrane, rift shoulder, hotspot/LIP}, `fold_dir` (2D unit vector), `peak_uplift` (maximum uplift rate the cell ever had) and optionally an `orogeny_count`. All are advected with the plate. A belt's identity (for naming and history) can be a connected-component label over cells sharing kind and an age bracket.
- Airy arithmetic with the usual densities (crust 2700, mantle 3300 kg/m³): root = 2700/(3300−2700) = 4.5× the elevation, and eroding a thickness T off the top lowers the surface by only about T × (1 − 2700/3300) ≈ 0.18 T, i.e. about 82 % is rebounded. So removing a 5 km high range takes about 5 × 5.5 ≈ 27 km of rock. (My calculation from standard densities; search results gave the model, not this number.)
- Consequence for the forge's Braun–Willett stage: if uplift is simply switched off for old belts, the range will be gone within a few simulated My — "old, low, rounded" would come out as "flat". Three fixes, combinable: (1) carry crust thickness and let erosion drive isostatic rebound (effective erodibility ÷ about 5.5); (2) add an incision threshold or lower K for old/cratonic rock; (3) do not run old belts through full-length erosion at all — give them a low residual uplift equal to the rebound and run to near steady state, which yields low, smooth, dendritic relief. Young belts get high uplift and are stopped far from steady state (sharp, high, immature drainage). Tzathas 2024's "time as a parameter" is the same idea.
- "Rounded" also needs hillslope diffusion: old ranges should get a higher diffusion-to-incision ratio, young ones lower; this is a per-cell erosion parameter the age map can drive.
- Linear rule of thumb (2,500 m − 5 m/My) hits zero at 500 My; an exponential with a 150–250 My e-folding time plus a floor of a few hundred metres for resistant lithology fits the Appalachian/Ural/Caledonide picture better. (Inference; the e-folding value is my guess bracketed by the 100+ My persistence figures above.)

### Gaps
- No primary source was read in full for erosion time constants; Baldwin et al.'s multipliers come from an abstract.
- Nothing citable was retrieved on the Scandinavian Caledonides, the Urals' present height, or the Tibetan plateau's crust thickness; the Urals/Caledonides comparison in the question is supported only in general terms.
- No source found that maps orogen TYPE to quantitative relief statistics (slope, drainage density) — Argudo et al. 2019 "Orometry-based terrain analysis and synthesis" is likely relevant but was not checked.

## 4. How do you get several ranges of different sizes per continent?

### Takeaway
Run long enough for more than one orogenic episode per continent and keep the old ones: every suture, accreted terrane and abandoned subduction margin leaves a belt whose age keeps counting, so a continent ends up with a young marginal cordillera, older interior sutures where former continents joined, and small accreted-arc belts. Cortial's terrane mechanism produces all three; belt size scales with terrane area and convergence speed.

### Cited Findings
- Collision footprint scales with the collider: radius `r = rc·sqrt(v/v0 · A/A0)` and surge `Δz ∝ Δc·A`, so an island arc makes a small low belt and a continent makes a wide high one (rc = 4,200 km is the upper bound). — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- A terrane is ANY connected continental region, "from small isolated islands to entire continents", and island arcs created by ocean–ocean subduction later accrete as terranes (Fig. 15 sequence 330→370 My). — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Subduction uplift reaches up to rs = 1,800 km inland with a peak at a control distance, and scales with relative speed and with the relief of what is being subducted — so one margin gives ranges of different height along strike. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- After suturing, the belt sits in a plate interior and only ages; rifting later cuts plates along NEW random fracture lines, so old sutures end up inside continents or split between two continents. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Real pattern to imitate: after an accretion, subduction jumps to the new coast or reverses polarity; broad plateaus come from repeated impacts of small fragments followed by a big fast collision; failed rifts stay as low weak zones that become river valleys and may re-rift; older ranges sit far from present boundaries, and old passive-margin ranges have wide coastal plains. — [Worldbuilding Pasta, Part Va](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2020/01/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-va.html)
- The Appalachians themselves record several successive episodes (first mountain building about 480 My ago, final collision about 300 My ago). — [Wikipedia, Geology of the Appalachians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_Appalachians)

### Inferences
- Why the current forge yields ONE range: static plates give each convergent boundary exactly one belt, all of the same age. Any time-stepped model with suturing and rifting fixes this structurally, even a crude one.
- Dials that control range count and size variety: (1) total simulated time relative to the rift interval — aim for 2–3 rift/collide episodes, i.e. 300–600 My at 2–5 My steps; (2) number of microcontinents and arcs seeded in the oceans (cheap source of small belts); (3) plate speed spread (fast colliders make Himalayan-style belts, slow ones Ural-style); (4) letting subduction polarity flip or jump after an accretion.
- To guarantee the outcome instead of hoping for it, score finished worlds (number of continents above a size, number of belts per continent by age class) and re-run with another seed when the score fails — the forge is offline and already has a quality gate, so rejection sampling is affordable at minutes per run.
- Rift shoulders and failed rifts give a fourth, non-collisional relief type (linear low valleys flanked by modest uplifts) for almost no cost: record the rift line when a plate splits, and record rift lines that are drawn but not opened.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how many distinct belts Cortial's runs produce per continent, or gives a tested λ0 value for rifting (the paper lists no number for λ0, β, ε or the cubic's control distance).

## 5. Flat-map issues: rectangle vs sphere-then-project

### Takeaway
PlaTec proves a flat raster with wrap-around works and is simple, but it wraps both axes (a torus) and only translates plates; a rectangle with wrapping east–west and hard polar edges needs an explicit rule for the top and bottom. The sphere approach (Cortial) has no edges but must be projected, and any projection onto a uniform-scale flat game map distorts shapes toward the poles.

### Cited Findings
- PlaTec/plate-tectonics wraps x AND y: neighbour lookup and plate stamping use modulo width and modulo height. — [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- Plates there carry a single 2D velocity vector (random angle, unit speed) and collide as point masses — rigid translation on the torus, which keeps plate rasters exact (integer offsets, no resampling loss). — [movement.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/movement.cpp)
- Cortial's sphere needs none of this but pays for it: Fibonacci point set, spherical Delaunay triangulation, per-plate BVH, and a global resample (3.6 s at 250k, 13 s at 500k points) every 10–60 steps. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Plate motion on a sphere is a rotation about an axis through the centre, so speed varies across a plate (`s = ω w × p`) and plates spin as they drift; a flat translation-only model has none of that. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Undiscovered Worlds added spherical worlds late (2023) on top of an originally flat generator. — [Undiscovered Worlds blog, 2023](https://undiscoveredworlds.blogspot.com/2023)

### Inferences
- Options for this project's rectangle (equator across the middle, poles at top/bottom):
  1. **Cylinder raster (recommended).** Wrap east–west; treat the top and bottom rows as the far side of an ocean: plates may slide past the edge, crust that leaves is destroyed (as if subducted) and gaps opening at the edge are filled with new oceanic crust (as if a ridge). Bias initial continents away from the edges. Motion = translation plus a small rotation about the plate centroid (Euler pole at finite distance in the plane) so plates do not all move like conveyor belts; resample rotating plates from their pristine source raster by inverse mapping (not incrementally) to avoid blur and mass loss.
  2. **Torus (PlaTec as is).** Simplest, but crust leaving the north edge re-enters at the south, which contradicts poles; only acceptable if a polar ocean band hides it.
  3. **Sphere then project.** Geometrically honest, but an equirectangular unwrap stretches east–west by 1/cos(latitude) (×2 at 60°, unbounded at the pole); since the game map has uniform scale, polar continents would be smeared and belts bent. Cropping to ±60° loses 13 % of the area and still has ×2 stretch at the edges. Worth it only if the game ever wants a globe view.
- The game map is a flat rectangle by design, so the "physically right" sphere buys realism the player can never see while costing mesh machinery; the cylinder keeps everything a 2D array (parallel per-row or per-plate with rayon), reuses the existing raster erosion stage directly, and has no projection step.
- Edge artefact to guard against on the cylinder: belts running parallel to and hugging the polar edge. Mitigations: an absorbing margin of a few percent of the height where uplift is faded out, and/or a prior that puts mostly oceanic plates there.
- With rotation in the plane, cells of a plate can map to the same world cell or leave gaps at the sub-cell level; PlaTec avoids this by never rotating. Inverse mapping (for each world cell covered by the plate's bounding box, look up the plate-local source cell) avoids holes.

### Gaps
- No source found that discusses polar-edge handling for a non-wrapping axis in a plate simulation; the cylinder rules above are my design proposal.
- No measured comparison of flat vs spherical results in any source.

## 6. How does the plate simulation feed an uplift/erosion model?

### Takeaway
The clean interface is the one Cordonnier 2016 and Schott 2023 established: the tectonic stage outputs an uplift-RATE map (plus control maps), and stream-power erosion turns it into relief. Cortial's formulas are already uplift rates (mm/y) per cell per step, so the tectonic stage can emit uplift maps directly — ideally a time series or age-binned set rather than one map.

### Cited Findings
- Uplift map → stream power → relief is the published pipeline: user-painted uplift map, stream graph, stream power equation (Cordonnier 2016); uplift as the authoring domain with interactive stream-power erosion, and the inverse — recovering an uplift map from a target elevation model (Schott 2023). — [Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf12820); [Schott et al. 2023](https://h-schott.github.io/publications/uplift/publi_uplift.html)
- Cortial's subduction term is a rate: u0 = 0.6 mm/y scaled by distance, speed and subducted relief, integrated at 2 My steps (so up to 1.2 km per step at full strength), against continental erosion of 0.03 mm/y × z/zc; collisions add a one-off surge. — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- Elevation follows crust thickness through isostasy (thicker crust floats higher, with a root). — [Wikipedia, Isostasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isostasy)
- Time can be an explicit input to stream-power terrain, from early transient to uplift/erosion steady state. — [Tzathas et al. 2024](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf15033)
- Without calibrated erosion a tectonic sim over-builds: Tectonics.js mountains filled every continental cell, then stood about 15 km high. — [Tectonics.js blog](https://github.com/davidson16807/tectonics.js/blob/master/_posts/2014-1-29-reddit-erosion-photosynthesis.md)

### Inferences
- Proposed hand-off from the tectonic stage (coarse grid, e.g. 512²–1024², bilinearly or noise-warped upsampled for the erosion grid):
  - `uplift_rate_now` — active uplift at the final step (young belts);
  - `inherited_relief` or `crust_thickness` → isostatic base elevation (old belts, plateaus, shields, shelves, ocean depth from crust age);
  - `orogeny_age`, `orogeny_kind`, `fold_dir` — drive erodibility K, hillslope diffusion, the uplift map's fine-scale anisotropy (ridges parallel to the fold direction), and later rock types/minerals for the game;
  - `crust_age` (ocean depth, craton flag), plate id and boundary kind (volcano and earthquake placement, world history).
- Two ways to couple, in rising cost: (A) one erosion run at the end on `uplift_rate_now` + isostatic base elevation, with K and diffusion modulated by age — cheapest, and the Braun–Willett stage stays as it is; (B) interleave: every N tectonic steps run a few implicit stream-power steps on the coarse grid with the current uplift, advecting topography with the plates — gives true multi-age relief (old drainage on old belts) but needs the eroded surface to be advected with the plates. Start with (A); the age map already lets (A) fake most of (B).
- Convert the collision surge to a rate (spread Δz over, say, 10–20 My after the event) so the erosion stage sees uplift rather than a step in elevation.
- Sea level should be solved after the fact (pick the level that gives the target land fraction), as PlaTec/WorldEngine effectively do with their sea-level quantile.

### Gaps
- No source gives a tested recipe for coupling a moving-plate simulation to Braun–Willett stream power; the published uplift-map work assumes a static, painted uplift.
- The exact Braun–Willett parameter ranges (K, m, n) suited to 2–5 My steps on a 512²–2048² planetary grid were not researched here (another researcher's scope).

## 7. Concrete numbers and the recommended approach

### Takeaway
Published working points: 40 plates, 2 My steps, about 125+ steps (250 My), 60k–500k samples, 0.2–1.9 s per step in single-digit minutes total (Cortial); 10 plates, up to 600 steps per cycle, 2 cycles, minutes at 1024² on 2000s hardware (PlaTec/WorldEngine). Recommendation: a Cortial-style rule set implemented on a PlaTec-style raster, on an east–west wrapping cylinder, starting from a supercontinent, recording age/type per cell and exporting uplift and age maps to the existing erosion stage.

### Cited Findings
- Cortial: δt 2 My; ≈250 My runs; 40 initial plates; land ratio 0.3; 60k/100k/250k/500k samples (102/78/50/35 km); 0.19/0.28/1.24/1.90 s per step plus 0.58/1.22/3.58/13.10 s per ocean-crust resample and 0.23/0.21/1.50/7.70 s per rifting; resample every 10–60 steps; max plate speed 100 mm/y; collision trigger at 300 km interpenetration; uplift reach 1,800 km (subduction) and up to 4,200 km (collision). — [Cortial et al. 2019](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf)
- PlaTec / WorldEngine: 10 plates; sea-level quantile 0.65; erosion every 60 steps; folding ratio 0.02; aggregation at 33 % overlap; 2 cycles; restart after at most 600 steps, or when energy < 15 % of peak, or 10 steps without continental collision; initial speed 1 cell/step. — [worldengine/plates.py](https://github.com/Mindwerks/worldengine/blob/master/worldengine/plates.py); [lithosphere.cpp](https://github.com/Mindwerks/plate-tectonics/blob/master/src/lithosphere.cpp)
- PlaTec run time (secondhand): 8–15 s at 128², 300–480 s at 1024², Pentium 4. — [GameDev.net thread](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/623145-terrain-generation-with-plate-tectonics/)
- Real plate speeds (hobbyist synthesis of literature): subducting ocean plates 10–20 cm/y at the extreme (India about 20 cm/y in the late Cretaceous), recently collided 5–10, continents with active margins 2–5, passive-margin continents under 1 cm/y; 1 cm/y ≈ 0.9° of arc per 10 My on Earth; supercontinent cycle 400–750 My. — [Worldbuilding Pasta, Part Va](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2020/01/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-va.html)
- Frozen Fractal's static generator uses about 15 plates from 6 continents. — [Frozen Fractal 2023](https://frozenfractal.com/blog/2023/11/13/around-the-world-2-plate-tectonics/)
- Tectonics.js: tens of thousands of cells in a browser. — [Tectonics.js blog](https://davidson16807.github.io/tectonics.js/blog)

### Inferences
**Recommended design (my synthesis; nothing below is a published, tested system):**
- Grid: 512×256 to 1024×512 tectonic cells on a cylinder (wrap in x), then upsample to the erosion grid. At a 40,000 km-wide world that is 40–80 km cells — the same band Cortial used (35–100 km). Tectonics does not benefit from finer cells; detail comes from erosion.
- Data: world arrays (plate id + the per-cell record of section 3) plus one source raster per plate with an affine transform (offset + angle). Each step: update transforms; inverse-map plates to the world grid; resolve overlaps and gaps; apply rules; every K steps "bake" (write the world arrays back into fresh per-plate rasters, as Cortial's global resample does) so that sutured/rifted crust changes owner. All loops are per cell and parallelise by rows or by plate.
- Rules, taken from Cortial with raster distance fields instead of mesh queries:
  - overlap, oceanic vs anything: the older/denser oceanic cell is consumed; a distance-to-front field (BFS or jump flooding on the overriding plate) drives `u0·f(d)·g(v)·h(z̃)`; set orogeny_kind = arc or Andean, age = 0, fold_dir ⟂ convergence;
  - overlap, continental vs continental beyond a threshold depth (300 km ≈ 4–8 cells): flood-fill the terrane, suture it to the other plate, apply the compact surge over radius r as an uplift pulse, kind = collisional (plateau variant when v or A is large or when several terranes hit the same margin), age = 0;
  - gaps: new oceanic crust, age 0, ridge profile; ocean depth from crust age;
  - slab pull: add to each plate's velocity a small pull toward its subducting edges, proportional to front length; ridge push optional;
  - rifting: Poisson per plate with rate ∝ area × continental fraction (and × time since assembly, to imitate thermal blanketing under supercontinents); fracture line = noise-warped Voronoi that AVOIDS high-`crust_age` (craton) cells; record rift shoulders and one failed arm;
  - hotspots: 10–20 fixed points stamping volcanic marks;
  - ageing: every step `orogeny_age += δt`, `crust_age += δt`.
- Time: δt 2–5 My, 150–300 steps (300–600 My ≈ most of a supercontinent cycle) starting from one supercontinent covering about 25–30 % of the map plus a few microcontinents; 8–20 plates at any time (rifting adds, suturing removes). Cap displacement at about 1 cell per step: at 60 km cells and 2 My, 1 cell/step = 30 mm/y; a 100 mm/y plate needs sub-steps or 3-cell moves with swept overlap tests.
- Cost estimate: each step is a handful of O(cells) passes plus a distance transform — on the order of tens of milliseconds at 1024×512 multi-threaded, so 300 steps should take seconds to tens of seconds, far inside the minutes budget, leaving room for rejection sampling on a quality score. (Estimate from operation counts; unmeasured.)
- Why not the alternatives: PlaTec as-is has no rifting, no ages, no rotation and ends when plates stall; Cortial as-is needs spherical meshing and a projection the flat game cannot use well; Tectonics.js-style field models are the most principled but least documented and least controllable; static boundary classification (Experilous, Frozen Fractal, the current forge) cannot produce multiple ages by construction.
- Known failure modes to plan tests for (from the sources): mountains everywhere without calibrated erosion (Tectonics.js); too many forced subductions (Cortial); land-area loss and continent deformation under resampling (second-system.de — check conservation of continental area per bake); ancient ocean crust that never subducts when plate motion is random rather than age-driven (Tectonics.js v1); supercontinent lock-in without rifting (Cortial's stated reason for rifting); smooth, featureless belts unless noise modulates uplift width and height (Frozen Fractal); translation-only motion and torus wrap (PlaTec).

### Gaps
- No published implementation of a Cortial-style model on a flat raster was found; the recommendation is untested and its run-time estimate is not measured.
- Cortial gives no values for λ0 (rift rate), ε (slab pull), β (fold update) or the control distance of the uplift profile; these must be tuned.
- PlaTec's own measured timings on modern hardware were not found.
